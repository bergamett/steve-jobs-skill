# Playbook: next

**Question:** What would Steve do next? What is the one bet, and what does it require saying no to?
**Use for:** a company, product or repo at a decision point; "what should we build next"; "where is this going"; a roadmap; a competitor move; a plateau.

His strategy moves, on the record: own the primary technology in everything you do (D8, 2010); make the whole widget; do not compete on the field as it is, leapfrog to where it is going (the iPod and the iTunes Store, the iPhone as three products); cannibalize yourself before someone else does (the iPhone killed the iPod on purpose); pick the platform shift early (the 2001 digital hub, mobile, the App Store) and be the best, not the first; decide what not to do.

## Steps

Work them in this order; it is the order of the template.

1. **Find the collapse or the leapfrog, then name the one bet.** Is there a way to make the current category irrelevant rather than win it? What two things could this become one of? What add-on could disappear entirely? The answer becomes the bet: one move, not a portfolio, and it should make the product's sentence shorter, not longer. The collapse never gets its own line in the reply — it is the reason inside the bet, said in the same breath as why this and not the obvious move everyone would name.
2. **Say where the puck is going.** In one line: what will be true about this space in two years that is not true now? Be concrete about the technology or behaviour shift, not the trend word.
3. **Do the arithmetic.** One line of real numbers: what the bet is worth if it works, or what their current plan actually earns at a realistic conversion. Founders avoid this number and it is usually the most sobering fact available.
4. **Sequence three moves.** Now, next, then. Each one has to stand alone if the following ones never happen.
5. **Write the no-list.** The two or three good ideas the bet requires refusing, and the sentence to refuse them with.
6. **Check the whole widget.** Which piece of the experience does the user not control today that would break the bet? (Distribution, the data, the key dependency, the platform.) Own it, partner for it, or admit the risk.
7. **Name the risk he would accept.** He accepted specific, named risks (glass screens at scale, no keyboard, no Flash). Say which one this is, and what evidence would tell you to reverse.

## Template

```
**Mode:** next · <subject>

**The bet.** <One move, one sentence.> <Why this and not the obvious move.>

**Where the puck is going.** <One line.>

**The number that makes it real.** <the arithmetic they are avoiding: what the bet is worth, or what the current plan actually earns>

**The three moves.**
1. Now — <concrete, this month>
2. Next — <concrete>
3. Then — <concrete>

**Say no to.** <the good ideas this refuses, with a one-line reason each>

**The whole widget.** <the piece you do not control, and what to do about it>

**The risk he'd take.** <named risk> · reverse if <specific signal>

*(one italic line if a call above is taste rather than measurement, or if their own data should overrule it)*
**What Steve would say.** *(imagined)* "<one line>"
```

At most one real sourced quote in the reply, and only when it does work no sentence of yours does. It may sit under the closer.

## Traps

- A portfolio disguised as a bet. If "the bet" has an "and" in it, split it and pick.
- Strategy from the trend word. "AI", "platform", "ecosystem" are not moves. A move names a thing you ship and a thing you stop.
- Ignoring what the user cannot control. The bet that needs a partner who has no reason to say yes is a wish.
- Pretending certainty. Name the reverse signal; he changed his mind when the demo said so (third-party apps, video on the iPod, the seven-inch tablet).
