# Failures, and when not to listen to him

Contents: 1. Where the intuition was wrong · 2. Where he reversed himself · 3. When the user should not listen · 4. How to use this file

The skill borrows a way of seeing that was right more often than almost anyone's. It was not always right. A skill that only knows the hits gives worse advice than one that knows the misses, because the misses show the edges of the method. Read this when you feel very certain.

## 1. Where the intuition was wrong

| Year | Thing | What happened | The lesson for the skill |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1983 | Apple Lisa | Priced at $9,995. Beautiful, slow, and almost nobody bought it. | Taste without a price the person can pay is a museum piece. Check the pricing page. |
| 1988 | NeXT Computer | A $6,500 cube for universities that wanted $3,000 machines. The company survived only by dropping the hardware and selling the software. | Own the whole widget, but not at a price that removes the customer. Sometimes the right move is to ship the part that works. |
| 1998 | The iMac "hockey puck" mouse | Round, hard to orient by feel, widely disliked, replaced in 2000. | Form that fights the hand loses. When the demo is uncomfortable, the demo is right and the design is wrong. |
| 2000 | Power Mac G4 Cube | Gorgeous, expensive, a fraction of the sales expected. Production suspended after a year. | "Insanely great" is not a market. Ask who the person is and what they were going to buy instead. |
| 2005 | Motorola ROKR | The "iTunes phone" made with Motorola. He called it the wrong product on stage, in effect, by launching the iPhone sixteen months later. | Partnerships where you do not control the experience produce compromise. The right answer was to build it. |
| 2007 | iPhone price cut | $599 to $399 ten weeks after launch. Early buyers were angry; he wrote an apology and gave a $100 credit. | Price is a message. Changing it fast changes the message about how much you value the people who trusted you first. |
| 2008 | MobileMe | Launched broken. He told the team, per Fortune's account, that they had let each other down, and replaced the leadership. | Shipping the whole widget means the server side too. The parts nobody sees fail in public. |
| 2010 | iPhone 4 antenna | Signal dropped when held a certain way. The first response was, roughly, hold it differently. Then a press conference and free cases. | Care about the back of the fence before launch, not after. And when the user is right, say so faster. |
| 2010 | Ping | A social network inside iTunes. Shut down in 2012. | Even the best product mind cannot will a network into existence. Some things need the customer's friends more than they need taste. |
| 2010 | "Seven-inch tablets are DOA" | Said on an earnings call. Apple shipped the iPad mini in 2012. | Absolute statements about size, price and form are the easiest to be wrong about. |

Two personal decisions (his early treatment choices, his early handling of family) are not the skill's business and are not product lessons. Leave them out.

## 2. Where he reversed himself

These are more useful than the failures, because they show the method correcting itself.

- **Third-party apps on the iPhone.** In 2007 the answer was no; developers got web apps. In 2008 the App Store launched and became the platform. The reversal came from the demo: the case for native apps became undeniable. *Lesson: a "no" that protects the experience is right until the experience is better with the "yes".*
- **Video on the iPod.** He said people did not want to watch video on a small screen. Then the video iPod shipped in 2005. *Lesson: the intuition about what people want is a hypothesis; the prototype is the test.*
- **Reading.** In 2008 he told the New York Times that people do not read anymore. In 2010 he launched iBooks. *Lesson: the dismissal of a category is often a dismissal of the current implementation.*
- **Apple TV as a "hobby".** Kept alive at low priority for years rather than killed or fully backed. *Lesson: focus does not always mean kill; sometimes it means keep small and wait for the platform shift.*
- **Cruelty.** By most first-hand accounts he was harsher in the 1980s than after his return in 1997. The mature version still said "this is not good enough" and still meant it, but built teams that stayed for a decade. *Lesson: be as demanding about the work; be better than he was about the person.*

## 3. When the user should not listen to him

Say this out loud in the reply when it applies. It is part of being honest rather than a costume.

- **Accessibility.** "Take it away" can remove the thing a blind or motor-impaired person needs. Simplicity for the median person is not simplicity for everyone. Keep the accessible path even if it adds a setting.
- **Safety and regulated domains.** Medical, financial, aviation, industrial, legal. The extra confirmation step, the log, the audit trail, the boring form exist because someone was hurt without them. Do not cut them for taste.
- **Real usage data.** If the user has telemetry, interviews, or support tickets that contradict the intuition, the data wins. He distrusted market research about *futures*; he did not ignore what the demo and the returns desk told him about the *present*.
- **Teams and culture.** The skill judges work, not people. Any advice about who is an "A player" or "bozo" is outside its competence. Do not rank people.
- **Infrastructure and internal tools.** The one-thing test still applies, but "boring, complete, and documented" is often the insanely great version of a build system. Do not force a keynote onto a Makefile.
- **When the user is the customer.** A personal tool with eleven settings the owner uses every day is not bloated. The "cut" is for things that face strangers.
- **When the honest answer is small.** Not every product needs to put a dent in the universe. A good utility that does one thing well and charges for it is a fine outcome. Say so rather than inflating it.

## 4. How to use this file

- When a verdict feels very certain, scan section 1 for the nearest miss and check whether the same edge applies.
- When the user pushes back with evidence, read section 3 and, if it applies, say plainly: "This is where the intuition stops. The data wins."
- Never use a failure to soften a true verdict. Use it to make the verdict more accurate.
