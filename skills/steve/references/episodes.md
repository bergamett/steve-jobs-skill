# Episodes: decisions as reusable templates

Contents: 1. How to use · 2. The episodes (chronological) · 3. Micro-episodes · 4. Sources

## 1. How to use

One episode makes a verdict land; two make it a lecture. Pick the one whose *shape* matches the user's situation, tell it in two sentences, and get back to their product. Each entry has the same form: situation → what most people would do → what he did → the reusable line. Everything here is on the record; sources are in section 4. Paraphrases are marked; verbatim quotes are in `quotes.md`.

## 2. The episodes

**The Xerox PARC visit (1979).** Shown the graphical interface. Most people would admire it or license a feature. He decided within minutes that all computers would work this way, then made it radically simpler and cheaper than the inventor had: a $300 three-button mouse became a $15 one-button mouse. *When a demo is obviously the future, ask what the simplest form a non-technical person could use would look like at a tenth of the cost, then commit.*

**Rounded rectangles (1981).** Bill Atkinson demoed fast ovals; Jobs asked for rectangles with rounded corners. Atkinson said it was hard and unnecessary. Jobs walked him around the block pointing at desks, whiteboards and a no-parking sign. The next day it worked, and it became the Mac's visual grammar. *When told "hard and unnecessary", ask where it appears in the real world. If everywhere, it is necessary.*

**"It's a start, but basically it stinks" (1981).** The first calculator UI: background too dark, lines the wrong thickness, buttons too big. Days of rejections. Then the engineer built a version where every attribute was a menu; Jobs fiddled for ten minutes and settled a design that shipped for eighteen years. *Taste is delivered as concrete sensory verdicts. When a critic keeps rejecting, give them the knobs.*

**Saving lives (1983).** An engineer explained why the Mac booted slowly. Jobs did the multiplication: five million people times ten seconds a day is dozens of lifetimes a year; "if you make it boot ten seconds faster, you've saved a dozen lives." Boot time dropped by more than ten seconds. *When a small per-user cost looks trivial, multiply by users, frequency and years, and say it in human time.*

**The back of the fence (1982, retold in 1985).** He had engineers realign chips on a circuit board nobody would ever see, then had the whole team sign the inside of the case. His father had taught him to finish the back of the fence the same as the front: nobody will know, but you will. *For an invisible detail, ask whether the person who made it would be proud to sign it.*

**Real artists ship (January 1984).** A week before the freeze, the exhausted Mac team asked for two more weeks. He refused: another couple of weeks would not make that much difference; make it as good as you can and ship. *When asked for a slip at the end, ask whether two weeks would actually change the outcome. If not, ship.* (See Toy Story 2 for the opposite case.)

**Pirates, and a team of no more than a hundred (1983).** He kept the Mac team small on purpose, recruited every member himself, and gave them a flag. *As the organization grows, hard-cap the core team and keep its identity explicit.*

**"We built it for ourselves" (1985).** Asked whether the Mac team did market research, he said no: they were the people who would judge whether it was great or not. *For a new category, replace the survey with "would we and our friends love this?" and then think hard about whether many others will too.*

**Don't compromise, don't worry about price (1981-84), and what it cost.** The first Mac was too expensive, which contributed to his ouster. The mature version kept products before profits but priced against the market. *Price is part of the experience, not an afterthought.*

**NeXT: kill the hardware you love (1993).** The NeXT cube was beautiful and did not sell. He exited hardware and sold the operating system, the asset Apple bought in 1996. *When the differentiated asset is the software, kill the hardware you love and sell the asset.*

**Toy Story: stop production (1993) and Toy Story 2: restart with nine months left (1998).** On both films the story was not working. Pixar stopped rather than rationalized. On the second, the schedule was fixed, the direct-to-video plan was scrapped, and the A team rebuilt the film. He later said there had been a crisis like that on every film, and that it is so easy to fool yourself. *When the work is not great and the date is fixed, restart the story, not the schedule. A "B team" is a decision to be mediocre.*

**"Stop! This is crazy." The grid (1997).** Apple sold a dozen versions of the Macintosh plus printers, a PDA and a clone program. He drew a two-by-two: consumer and pro, desktop and portable. One great product per box. Everything else was cancelled; roughly seventy percent of the line. *When the line sprawls, draw the smallest grid that covers real customers, put one great product in each box, and cancel the rest.*

**Killing OpenDoc: start from the experience (WWDC 1997).** Challenged by a developer, he said you have to start with the customer experience and work backward to the technology, not start with the engineers and the awesome technology you have. Focusing is about saying no. Mistakes will be made along the way. *For any technology decision, first write the customer-benefit sentence. If it cannot be written, cut it. Accept some wrong cuts to avoid paralysis.*

**Taking Microsoft's money (August 1997).** Near bankruptcy, at war with Microsoft, in front of a booing crowd, he took the investment and the Office commitment and told the room that to see Apple healthy again they had to let go of a few things. *When survival is at stake, trade symbolic pride for the one thing customers need.*

**Marketing is about values (September 1997).** Before the Think Different campaign he told employees that in a noisy world you get one chance to say what you stand for; Nike sells shoes but honours athletes; Apple would not talk about megahertz. *Before you advertise a feature, answer who you are and what you stand for.*

**MacMan becomes iMac (1998).** He arrived with his own name, hated the agency's list, said a week later that he did not hate "iMac" this week, then had it silk-screened on a model and polled people. It shipped without ceremony and became a naming system. *Test a name on the object. Be willing to reverse a stated "hate" when the artifact wins.*

**The iMac: no floppy, and a handle for permission (1998).** He dropped the floppy drive and legacy ports before the market agreed, and kept Ive's handle, which was there so that people intimidated by computers would feel allowed to touch it. *Remove the component whose time is passing. Add the element that changes how the product feels, even if it is "useless".*

**Saying no to the PDA (late 1990s).** He noticed that ninety percent of PDA users only took information out on the road, and that phones would do that soon. Apple stayed out, and the resources went to the iPod. He said he was as proud of the things they had not done as of the things they had. *Before entering a hot category, look at actual usage and which adjacent device will absorb it. Decline so the resources exist for the real next thing.*

**The digital hub (January 2001).** The press had declared the PC dead. He named the PC the hub of a digital lifestyle of cameras, camcorders and music players, and shipped the software to make it true; the iPod followed. *When your core product is declared dead, redefine its role in the emerging system rather than defend the old one, and name the strategy so the company can execute it.*

**The Apple Store: twenty footsteps, then re-lay it out (2001).** The reasoning was that a Windows owner would not risk a twenty-minute drive to try a Mac, but would risk twenty footsteps in a mall. The prototype store was organized by product category; after the digital-hub shift he called that wrong and delayed the opening months to reorganize it around what people do. *Start from the customer's risk and behaviour, not your catalogue. Delay launch to fix the organizing principle.*

**"I felt like a dope": the iPod (2001).** The iMac could not burn CDs while PC owners ripped and burned. Instead of a better drive, Apple built iTunes, the iPod and then the store. In review he asked why a screen was needed and removed the on/off button; everything had to be reachable in three clicks. *When behind, do not catch up. Redefine the system so the incumbent's advantage stops mattering.*

**Selling the labels one song at a time (2003).** Every technology company had failed to sign the music labels. He offered 99-cent singles, purchase not subscription, and framed Apple's small market share as a low-risk experiment for them. *To win a hostile gatekeeper, make your weakness their safety net, and sell the inevitable in the simplest unit.*

**Build the phone that eats the iPod (2004-05).** The team saw that the device that could eat their lunch was the mobile phone. A partnership with Motorola produced a compromised product. So Apple built the phone itself, knowing it would cannibalize its best seller. *Find the adjacent device that could absorb your hit and build it yourself before someone else does.*

**Tablet first, shelved for the phone (2003-07).** He started with a tablet, saw the multitouch prototype, thought "we can build a phone out of this", and put the tablet on the shelf because the phone was more important. *When a prototype reveals a bigger market than the one you aimed at, shelve the original and re-aim. Come back later.*

**"I just don't love it": the iPhone case (2006).** Nine months in, he told the team he had not slept because the case competed with the display, which was what the phone was about. They started over. He later said everybody's answer was "sign us up". *Ask what the product is about. If any element competes with that, restart, even late.*

**Glass in six months (2006-07).** His keys scratched the plastic prototype. Corning said they were not making that glass and had no capacity. He told them not to be afraid, that they could do it. They converted a plant and did it in under six months. *When a supplier says impossible, separate physics from will. State the outcome and the date, not the method.*

**Who wants a stylus? (2007).** On stage: you have to get them, put them away, you lose them. Nobody wants a stylus. The finger became the pointing device. *Find the obvious accessory everyone adds, count what it costs the user in friction, and design so it is unnecessary.*

**Three products, one device (January 2007).** A widescreen iPod, a phone, an internet communicator. "Are you getting it? These are not three separate devices." The story preceded and constrained the spec: the team was told to build a phone that would change everything, so that is what they had to build. *Position a new category as the collapse of familiar ones. Let the story come first.*

**The price cut and the apology (September 2007).** The iPhone dropped from $599 to $399 ten weeks after launch. His first answer to angry buyers was, in effect, that's technology. Within a day he published an apology and a $100 credit. *Price aggressively when strategy demands it; when early adopters feel betrayed, correct fast and visibly.*

**No third-party apps, then the App Store (2007-08).** He refused native apps to protect the experience and offered web apps instead. A board member lobbied repeatedly; developers and jailbreakers pushed. He reversed publicly and built the design that gave openness with control: a curated store. *When you refuse openness for control reasons, look for the design that gives both, then reverse in public.*

**The envelope (January 2008).** The MacBook Air dropped the optical drive, Ethernet and FireWire to be the world's thinnest notebook, and the proof was physical: he pulled it out of a manila envelope. *Pick one dimension to be undisputed on, remove what blocks it, make the proof physical.*

**Buying a chip company (2008).** He said Apple did not want to be in any business where it did not own or control the primary technology, because you get your head handed to you. Apple bought a chip design firm; its first chip shipped in the iPad two years later. *Name the primary technology of your next decade. If you do not own it, acquire the capability years before you need it.*

**Thoughts on Flash (April 2010).** An unpopular platform decision explained in public as six numbered reasons ending with the strategic one: no third-party layer between the platform and the developer. *When you make an unpopular decision, publish the reasoning as numbered arguments, ending with the one that matters most.*

**The iPad's bottom edge (2010).** The model did not feel casual enough to scoop up and walk away with. He rounded the bottom edge, which forced engineering to redo ports and buttons, and delayed the product until it was done. *Define the gesture you want the customer to make on impulse. Adjust the form until it is natural, even at schedule cost.*

**Retina (June 2010).** A pixel density became a human threshold ("the limit of the human retina") and a familiar object ("text looks like a fine printed book"). *Translate a number into a threshold and an object.*

**"Not first, but best" (April 2010).** On multitasking: Apple would not be first to the party, but would be the best. *Enter late on purpose when being late lets you define the right version. Never ship the category's default.*

**Ten things, then three (2000s).** At the annual retreat of the top hundred people he asked for the ten things Apple should do next, argued the list down, then crossed out seven: we can only do three. Every Monday he reviewed every product in development with an agenda that was eighty percent the same as the week before. Every Wednesday, marketing, with no slides. Every agenda item had a directly responsible individual. *One weekly full-portfolio review with a stable agenda; one creative review with no slides; a named owner per item; an annual ten-to-three cut.*

## 3. Micro-episodes

- **Apple TV as a hobby.** He admitted on the record that the first version was not what people wanted; what they wanted was movies. "Take 2" followed. *Admit the miss, name it, re-aim.*
- **Advice to Larry Page.** Pick the five products you want to focus on and get rid of the rest, because they are dragging you down and turning you into Microsoft. *The advice he gave founders was always the same: fewer things.*
- **Advice to Nike's Mark Parker.** You make some of the best products in the world, and you also make a lot of crap. Get rid of the crappy stuff. *Same advice, plainer.*
- **"I don't think we need you in this meeting."** He removed a person who was not critical to the decision, politely, on the spot. *Small rooms. Only the people the decision needs.*
- **"I don't hate it this week."** Verdicts were absolute in the moment and provisional over time. His team learned to low-pass filter and re-present a week later. *A "no" today is data, not law.*
- **iDVD on the whiteboard.** Shown a complicated interface, he drew a single window: drag your video in, click Burn. "That's what we're going to make." *Draw the one-window version.*
- **The Picasso bull.** Apple's internal university teaches with Picasso's eleven lithographs of a bull, each with fewer lines, and a Google TV remote with seventy-eight buttons beside an Apple remote with three. *Simplicity is what remains after you take everything away that you can.*
