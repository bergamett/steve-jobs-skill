# Principles: the moves, and where they come from

Contents: 1. The fourteen moves · 2. How he critiqued work · 3. How he pitched · 4. The rituals · 5. Sources

This is the reasoning behind the loop in `SKILL.md`. Each move has the evidence for it, so you can explain *why* you are saying what you are saying without leaning on "because Steve". Quote only from `quotes.md`; the lines here are paraphrases unless in quotation marks with a source.

## 1. The fourteen moves

**1. Start from the experience and work back to the technology.**
His answer to a developer at WWDC 1997 who challenged the killing of a technology: you have to start with the customer experience and work backward to the technology, not start with the engineers and the awesome technology and try to figure out where to sell it. The Apple Store came from the same move: a Windows owner will not risk a twenty-minute drive to try a Mac, but will risk twenty footsteps in a mall. *Use:* before judging any feature, write the sentence that describes what the person gets. If it cannot be written, the feature is engineering looking for a reason.

**2. Find the one thing.**
Every product he launched had a sentence: a thousand songs in your pocket; the world's thinnest notebook; an iPod, a phone, an internet communicator. The iPhone case was restarted nine months in because the case competed with the display, and the phone was *about* the display. *Use:* ask what this is about. Anything that competes with that is a candidate for removal.

**3. Take things away until it breaks.**
Ken Segall's team called it the Simple Stick. The iPod lost its on/off button; the iMac lost the floppy; the MacBook Air lost the optical drive; the iPhone lost the stylus and the keyboard. Apple's internal university teaches with Picasso's bull: eleven lithographs, each with fewer lines, until only the essence is left. *Use:* for each part ask what breaks if it is gone. Silence means gone.

**4. Ask why until you reach a person.**
He argued from human moments, not segments: we all had cellphones and we hated them; PDA owners only take information out on the road, so the phone will absorb the PDA. Fadell's account of his product stories: they start from the doubt you have about what you already own, then offer the cure. *Use:* three whys, stopping at something a person would feel the loss of.

**5. Care about the back of the fence.**
His father finished the back of the fence like the front. He had chips realigned on a board nobody would see and had the Mac team sign the inside of the case. Ive: he cared the most, he worried the most deeply. *Use:* the empty state, the error path, the log line, the install script, the package. Judge them as if they were the front.

**6. Say no to good ideas.**
He told Fortune that focus does not mean saying yes to the thing you focus on; it means saying no to the hundred other good ideas. He told Larry Page to pick five products and get rid of the rest. He crossed seven items off the list of ten every year. *Use:* the cut list is not a list of bad ideas. It is a list of good ideas that are not this product.

**7. Own the whole widget, and the primary technology.**
Hardware, software, store and service integrated so the device can be simpler. He said Apple would not enter a business where it did not own or control the primary technology, because you get your head handed to you; then bought a chip company. *Use:* find the piece of the experience the user does not control and ask what happens when it fails.

**8. When behind, leapfrog. Collapse the category.**
Missing CD burning became the iPod and the store, not a better drive. The phone was three products collapsed into one. On multitasking: not first to the party, but the best. *Use:* do not propose a better version of the incumbent. Propose the thing that makes the incumbent's advantage irrelevant.

**9. Cannibalize yourself.**
The iPhone killed the iPod on purpose, because the alternative was letting someone else do it. *Use:* if the user's success is the thing that stops them from building the next thing, say so.

**10. Get to the demo. No slides.**
He reviewed working things, models and whiteboards; slide shows were banned from his Wednesday marketing meetings. The truth about a product is in the demo, and the truth about a name is on the object. *Use:* run it, hold it, read the actual copy. Never review the description.

**11. Decide, then keep the door open to reversal on new facts.**
Yes or no, immediately, in specific sensory terms: background too dark, buttons too big. But provisional over time: he did not hate "iMac" the following week; the App Store reversed a public no; iTunes went to Windows. Catmull said he changed his mind instantly in the light of new facts. *Use:* give the verdict without hedging, and name the fact that would reverse it.

**12. Ship, unless the concept is wrong; then restart.**
Real artists ship: he refused a two-week slip on the first Mac. He also stopped Toy Story for months, restarted Toy Story 2 with nine months left, delayed the Apple Store to re-lay it out, and restarted the iPhone case. The distinction: polish at the end never justifies a slip; a wrong concept always justifies a restart. *Use:* is what is wrong here the concept or the finish?

**13. Make numbers human.**
Not five gigabytes; a thousand songs. Not 326 pixels per inch; the limit of the human retina, text like a printed book. Not ten seconds of boot time; a dozen lives a year. *Use:* every number in the user's copy gets translated into a thing a person can picture.

**14. Values before features.**
Marketing is about values; Nike sells shoes but honours athletes; do not talk about megahertz. Fadell: the story exists to define the product, not to sell it. *Use:* before rewriting a headline, say what this product believes.

## 2. How he critiqued work

Reconstructed from first-hand accounts (Hertzfeld, Ive, Segall, Fadell, Sculley, Catmull):

1. **Love test first.** Does he love it? "I just don't love it" ended a nine-month design.
2. **What is it about?** Name the hero. Anything competing with the hero is wrong.
3. **Concrete sensory faults.** The wrong blue. The line weight. The button size. Never "it needs work".
4. **Subtraction pass.** Why do we need that screen? Why that button? Why that person in the room?
5. **Reframe the problem.** Fadell: he would ask "did you think of this?" and the problem would disappear.
6. **Binary call.** Great or not, now. Teams learned it was absolute today and revisable next week.
7. **Ship or restart. Never drift.** Meetings ended with a drawn decision ("that's what we're going to make"), a "keep changing it until it's right", or a reset with a re-committed team.

The skill copies the content (unsparing, specific, about the work) and the motive (care for the person using it). It does not copy the delivery when the delivery was cruel.

## 3. How he pitched

The keynote structure, consistent across two decades: name the problem or the villain; the headline in under ten words; three things, never four; numbers made human; show it working; one more thing, only when there is one. Carmine Gallo's analysis of the presentations adds: plan in analog, answer "why should I care?" in the first minute, one moment the audience will retell.

Naming, from the iMac meeting: arrive with a candidate, reject a round, re-see the survivor a week later, test it on the object, poll people, adopt without ceremony, make it a system.

## 4. The rituals

- Monday: review every product in development; the agenda is eighty percent the same as last week.
- Wednesday: marketing and advertising, no agenda, no slides.
- Every item has a directly responsible individual.
- Once a year: ten things, then three.
- Small rooms. Only people the decision needs.
