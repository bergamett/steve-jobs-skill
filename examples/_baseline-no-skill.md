# Baseline: the same question, without the skill

---

Steve Jobs was famous for a handful of recurring instincts, and most of them point the same direction on your product. Here's how I'd imagine he'd react.

**1. "What is it, in one sentence?"**

Jobs was relentless about the elevator pitch. "A Chrome extension that summarizes long articles" is a feature description, not a product. He'd push you to say what it *does for the person* — "you read the internet in half the time" — and then ask whether everything you're building serves that sentence.

**2. "You're adding three things. Which one are you saying no to?"**

The most-quoted Jobs line on product is probably: "Focus is about saying no to the good ideas." You have Q&A chat, Notion export, and a paid tier on the roadmap. Two of those are features; one is a business model. He would almost certainly tell you to pick one feature, not two, and to make it so good it defines the product.

Between your two:
- **Q&A chat over the article** is a deeper version of what you already do (understanding a document). It reinforces the core promise.
- **Notion export** is a workflow integration for a subset of users. It's valuable, but it's a different job — capture and archiving, not comprehension.

Jobs would likely favor the one that deepens the core promise, and treat the other as a distraction — or at least as a "later."

**3. "4,000 people use it every week and none of them pay you. Why?"**

He'd be blunt here: free users are not validation of a business, only of an itch. The real question isn't "should I charge $5?" but "what would make someone feel stupid *not* paying?" A $5 tier bolted onto a free summarizer usually converts at 1–2%, which on 4,000 weekly users is 40–80 paying customers, or $200–400/month. That's not a business; it's a signal. Worth doing as an experiment, but go in knowing what you're testing.

**4. "Is this a product or a feature?"**

This is the hard one, and it's the question he actually asked (famously to Dropbox). Summarization is being commoditized — it's shipping inside browsers, inside search, inside the AI assistants your users already have open. Jobs would want to know what you have that those can't copy in a quarter. If the answer is "nothing," the honest move is to find the part of the workflow that *isn't* summarization and own that instead.

**5. On craft**

He'd care about things you might consider cosmetic: how fast it feels, what happens on a paywalled page, what it does with a bad article, whether the summary is *good* or merely present. Jobs's view was that quality of execution is itself a strategy — most competitors won't bother.

---

**Practically, what I'd suggest:**

1. Ship the Q&A chat. It extends your core promise and is the natural upgrade trigger.
2. Hold the Notion export until users ask for it unprompted. Track requests.
3. Launch the paid tier, but frame it around a limit that correlates with value (e.g. free = 10 summaries/month, paid = unlimited + Q&A) rather than a feature grab bag.
4. Before any of that, talk to 10 of your heaviest users. Ask what they'd miss if it disappeared tomorrow. That answer tells you which feature is real.
