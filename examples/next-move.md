# Example: `/steve` on a product at a plateau

**The ask.** "What would Steve Jobs say about my product?" — a Chrome extension that summarizes articles, 4,000 weekly users, no revenue, three features planned.

---

**Mode:** next · a summarizer at 4,000 users

*Working from your message — I have not seen the listing, the first run, or a retention curve. With nothing to hold, a verdict would end up reviewing the category instead of your product, so: `next`.*

**The bet.** Stop summarizing articles. Start remembering them: everything you have ever read, and you can ask it anything. Not the obvious move, which is your list — three verbs bolted onto one article. The article is the wrong unit. The reader is the unit, and 4,000 people have been handing you their reading every week for months. The browser cannot get that.

**Where the puck is going.** In two years "summarize this page" is a free button in Chrome and in every assistant your users already have open. A summary is not a product. It is a keystroke.

**The number that makes it real.** Free extensions convert at one or two in a hundred. Take the good end: 80 people times five dollars is $400 a month, before Stripe and before the model bill for the other 3,920 you serve for nothing. Your plan quietly asks for 100,000 weekly users — twenty-five times what you have — just to clear $10k a month. Memory is the only thing on your list a person would pay five dollars a month not to lose.

**The three moves.**
1. Now — keep every article you summarize, searchable, one key: "what have I read about X?" Free to the 4,000. Watch whether anyone goes back.
2. Next — charge for memory, not summaries. Free forgets after 30 days, five dollars remembers forever. Summaries stay free; you cannot sell what Chrome is about to give away.
3. Then — make the memory answerable where people write, not only where they read.

**Say no to.**
- The Notion export. If they have to export it, you have admitted your product is not where their reading lives.
- Q&A on a single article. The same feature scoped too small, and it dies the day the browser ships it.
- A better summarizer. That race is decided by someone else's model.

**The whole widget.** You own neither the model nor the browser, and either can take your feature away in a release note. What you can own is the reading history and an email address attached to it. You have 4,000 users and, I would guess, no way to reach one of them. Fixing that costs less than any feature on your list.

**The risk he'd take.** Storing what people read. Some will leave over it. Make it opt-in, say plainly where it sits, take the loss. Reverse if 60 days after the archive ships fewer than one in five weekly users ever asks about something older than a week — then the memory is not real, and the honest outcome is a small one: a good utility that does one thing and charges for it.

*This is taste, not measurement. You have four thousand people's behaviour and I do not. If it says they never look back at an old article, your data wins over everything above.*

**What Steve would say.** *(imagined)* "You built a button. Next year the browser ships that button for free. Build the thing that remembers what I read — that is the product."
